AI Art and Game Dev – An Honest Take

AI art tools are everywhere now. Every few weeks there's a new model, a new controversy, or a new game studio announcing they're using them. Here's an honest look at what they're actually useful for in game development — and what the noise obscures.

What AI Art Tools Exist

Choose a workflow before choosing a model: generate visual references, edit an existing image, or produce assets for a build. Check whether a tool supports reference images, selected-area edits, transparency, and the export dimensions you need. Features and plan limits change, so test a small asset set before committing a project to one service.

What They're Actually Useful For in Game Dev

Let's be specific, because the answer varies a lot by use case.

Concepting and Mood Boards

Use a mood board to compare lighting, palette, composition, and material choices. Write down which parts of each reference matter, then turn those observations into an art brief. A collection of attractive images alone does not define a repeatable production style.

Texture Generation

For 3D games especially, AI tools can generate tileable textures for environments quickly. Tools like Stable Diffusion with specific settings can produce stone, wood, concrete, and organic textures that work well as a base for manual cleanup.

Placeholder Art

During prototyping, you need something in the game to understand how it feels. AI-generated placeholders can fill that role — fast, good enough to iterate against, thrown away before shipping.

Background Elements with Low Visual Importance

A distant cityscape, a decorative border, a pattern on a wall that the player never looks at closely — these are lower-stakes uses where AI-generated art causes less friction.

A Small Asset Trial Before You Commit

  1. Write a brief: specify the asset's purpose, dimensions, background, palette, and required views.
  2. Make a complete sample: for a character, include an idle pose, a movement pose, and a back view rather than three unrelated portraits.
  3. Place it in the game: view it at native resolution against a representative background.
  4. Request one revision: change a specific detail and compare what else changed.
  5. Record the cost: include generation, selection, editing, export, and engine integration time. Compare that total with drawing or commissioning the same sample.

Keep the brief and the accepted source files together. If the sample fails, narrow the task or choose another production method before producing a whole asset library.

Where AI Art Falls Short

Character Consistency

Test character consistency across the actual poses your game needs: front, side, back, movement, and damage states. Check silhouette, costume details, proportions, and colour placement at the final on-screen size. A convincing single portrait does not demonstrate that an entire animation set will work together.

Specific Art Styles

If you want art that looks like your game's established visual identity, AI tools struggle to replicate it consistently. They can approximate influences, but they're poor at "more of this specific thing I already have."

UI and Readable Elements

Buttons, menus, HUDs, text overlays — AI tools are unreliable here. Generated UI often has placeholder-looking text, inconsistent sizing, and visual noise that makes the interface harder to read, not easier.

Anything That Needs Iteration Feedback

Selected-area editing is available in tools such as ChatGPT Images. You can request a change to an arm or another part of an image, but inspect the rest of the result too. Compare alignment, unchanged details, transparency, and palette after each edit; budget time for manual cleanup rather than assuming each revision is production-ready.

The Controversy — What It's Actually About

Training sources and licensing vary by model. For example, Adobe describes its Firefly models as trained on licensed material and public-domain content. Read the documentation for the specific model you use, including any partner model offered inside another product. A service permitting commercial use does not by itself settle every question about a particular output.

Commissioned art also buys collaboration: feedback, source files, a consistent visual direction, and agreed revisions. Compare the complete workflow cost, including cleanup and integration, rather than comparing a generation fee with an entire art commission.

For release planning, use the current Steamworks Content Survey as the source for disclosure requirements. Keep a record of which shipped assets use generative tools so the survey can be completed accurately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should indie developers use AI art in their games?

Start with a limited test: choose one asset type, define acceptance criteria, and measure the cleanup needed before integration. For shipped assets, check the model terms and store requirements and keep an asset record. Internal concept experiments and player-facing content are different uses; describe your actual use accurately.

Is AI-generated art allowed on Steam?

Steam accepts games using AI-generated content subject to its review and rules. The Content Survey distinguishes pre-generated content from content generated while the game runs; live generation also requires information about safeguards against illegal content. Check the current Steamworks documentation when preparing your submission.

What is AI art actually good for in game development?

Concepting and mood boarding (generating visual references before commissioning real art), texture generation for 3D assets, placeholder art during early development, background elements with low visual importance, and rapid prototyping. It's less reliable for main characters, UI, or anything that needs to look consistent across many frames or angles.

The Honest Verdict

AI art tools are useful in game development for specific tasks — concepting, placeholders, textures, reference generation. Treating them as a replacement for commissioned or in-house art for a commercial game is a different question, with real trade-offs in quality, consistency, community reception, and ethics.

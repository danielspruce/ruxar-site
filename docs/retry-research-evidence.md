# Retry research: evidence ledger and handoff

Prepared and sources checked: 2026-10-22 (Australia/Sydney).

## Editorial brief

- Reader: Steam players deciding whether they enjoy the retry loop in difficult platformers.
- Intent: explain the appeal of another attempt using motivation and persistence research, with adjacent skill findings clearly qualified.
- Candidate queries (editorial suggestions, no measured search demand): why do people enjoy difficult games; why do I keep retrying hard games; video games and persistence; satisfaction of mastering a game.
- Contribution: synthesis distinguishing association, training findings, and our interpretation for 2D platformers. Not original research or a systematic review.
- Overlap check: `why-hard-games-are-good-for-you.html` is an existing broad opinion piece about benefits. The new page addresses the narrower retry/motivation question and documents primary sources and limits. Added a related-reading link from that article; did not reproduce its dopamine, flow, emotional-regulation, or general cognitive-benefit claims.

## Evidence ledger

All findings concern original study samples, not Steam's global player population. Experimental observation dates were not established from the accessed material; publication years must not be described as collection years. No pooled effects, averages, or original calculations appear in the article.

### R1 — Competence, autonomy, enjoyment, and return motivation

- Claim: perceived competence and autonomy were associated with enjoyment; the paper also reports links with future play motivation and intuitive controls.
- Value/unit: four studies; qualitative direction of associations, no effect size asserted.
- Population/geography: participants in three game-play studies and a multiplayer community survey; exact sample sizes and locations not established from abstract.
- Observation period: unspecified.
- Publication date: 2006-11-29.
- Source: Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski, *The Motivational Pull of Video Games: A Self-Determination Theory Approach*.
- URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8
- Exact location: publisher Abstract, descriptions of Studies 1–3 and Study 4.
- Access date/status: 2026-10-22; verified against publisher abstract; full article requires subscription on this route.
- Limits: no claim that more difficulty causes more enjoyment; no iZBOT trial. Platformer mastery example is labelled interpretation.
- Article locations: introduction, research table, #mastery, #source-1.

### R2 — Gaming and persistence

- Claim: greater reported game use was associated with longer time spent on unsolved anagrams/riddles; causality was not established.
- Value/unit: 102 undergraduate participants; no causal effect estimate asserted.
- Population/geography: educational psychology students at Florida State University, US; 83 women (81%).
- Observation period: unspecified; cross-sectional assessment, self-reported gaming history.
- Publication date: 2013 volume; accepted 2012-07-03 (do not confuse these dates).
- Source: Ventura, Shute, and Zhao, *The relationship between video game use and a performance-based measure of persistence*, Computers & Education 60, 52–58.
- URL: https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/pbmp.pdf
- Exact location: section 4.1.5 / journal p.54 (PDF p.3), sample; Table 1 and Results / journal pp.54–55, association; section 7 / journal p.56 (PDF p.5), explicit reverse-causality limitation.
- Access date/status: 2026-10-22; verified original author-hosted PDF.
- Limits: self-reported gaming, narrow student sample, no assigned game training, no evidence that a hard platformer creates resilience at school/work.
- Article locations: introduction, research table, #persistence, #source-2.

### R3 — Undermining competence and frustration-related responses

- Claim: conditions that impeded perceived competence increased measured aggressive responses in the reported studies.
- Value/unit: seven studies; no pooled effect or population-wide aggression claim asserted.
- Population/geography: study-specific participants; exact counts and settings not established from accessed abstract.
- Observation period: unspecified.
- Publication date: March 2014 issue; online first 2013-12-30.
- Source: Przybylski, Deci, Rigby, and Ryan, *Competence-impeding electronic games and players' aggressive feelings, thoughts, and behaviors*.
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24377357/
- Exact location: Abstract, seven-study findings and competence hypothesis; Erratum notice.
- Access date/status: 2026-10-22; verified original indexed abstract.
- Correction: PubMed records an erratum affecting Edward L. Deci's name and Figure 1. Article copy uses the corrected author name and makes no claim from that figure.
- Limits: study-specific aggression measures are not criminal violence; no claim that every difficult game has these effects. Readable-failure recommendation is editorial interpretation.
- Article locations: research table, #frustration, #source-3.

### R4 — Visuomotor training

- Claim: participants improved tested visuomotor control after training with driving or first-person shooter games compared with non-action game training.
- Value/unit: reported training doses of 5 or 10 hours; no universal minimum dose or clinical benefit claimed.
- Population/geography: non-action gamers in training experiments; exact samples/settings not established from abstract.
- Observation period: unspecified.
- Publication date: 2016-07-08 online, August 2016 issue.
- Source: Li, Chen, and Chen, *Playing Action Video Games Improves Visuomotor Control*.
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27485132/
- Exact location: Abstract, training/control comparison and dose.
- Access date/status: 2026-10-22; verified original indexed abstract.
- Limits: not a 2D platformer trial, no guaranteed transfer or lasting iZBOT benefit.
- Article locations: research table, #skills, #source-4.

### R5 — Dual-task and switching training

- Claim: action-game trainees improved tested coordination of simultaneous/sequential tasks compared with puzzle-game trainees.
- Value/unit: 15 hours of practice; no general intelligence or all-context multitasking claim.
- Population/geography: non-gamers in training study; exact samples/settings not established from abstract.
- Observation period: unspecified.
- Publication date: 2012-03-14 online, May 2012 issue.
- Source: Strobach, Frensch, and Schubert, *Video game practice optimizes executive control skills in dual-task and task switching situations*.
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22426427/
- Exact location: Abstract, experienced-player comparison and 15-hour training finding.
- Access date/status: 2026-10-22; verified original indexed abstract.
- Limits: tested tasks; no established transfer from hard-platformer play. PubMed lists a methodological commentary, so wording stays narrow and does not present a field-wide consensus.
- Article locations: research table, #skills, #source-5.
